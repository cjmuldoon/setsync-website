How to Coach Lifters Remotely Without Losing the Detail

11 October 2026 · 7 min read

Remote coaching lives or dies on the quality of what the athlete sends you and the precision of what you send back. Get both right and the gap matters surprisingly little. Get either wrong and you are guessing at technique from a shaky clip filmed from across the gym.

Standardise what the athlete films

The most valuable thing a remote coach can do in week one is specify the filming setup and refuse to budge on it. Send every athlete the same brief:

  • Camera square to the side, at hip crease height for squats
  • Three to four metres back, no zoom
  • Same spot every session, marked on the floor
  • Film the full set, not just the rep that felt bad
  • State the load and the rep number in the file name or caption

Consistency matters more than perfection here. A slightly imperfect angle used every single week is far more useful than a perfect angle used once.

Ask for the whole set

Athletes naturally send the rep they thought looked worst, which is the least representative sample available. Technique under fatigue is the thing you actually want to coach, and that only shows up across a full set.

Watching reps one, three and five of the same set tells you whether a fault is a technique problem or a conditioning problem. Those need different answers.

Give feedback anchored to a frame

Written feedback like keep your chest up is almost useless at distance, because the athlete does not know which part of which rep you mean.

Feedback that works is pinned to a specific moment: a comment attached to the exact frame where the hips outrun the chest, with a line or angle drawn on it. The athlete sees the position, not a description of it.

Timestamped comments and frame pinned annotations exist for this reason. A single arrow on the right frame replaces several paragraphs of text that get read once and misinterpreted.

Limit yourself to one cue

A video review that lists six faults produces zero changes. Pick the one that, if fixed, makes the others smaller, and say only that.

Most technique faults are downstream of one or two root causes. A forward bar drift out of the hole and an early hip rise are usually the same problem described twice. Coach the cause.

Keep the record, not just the conversation

The value of remote coaching compounds when you can show an athlete week one against week ten at the same load. That requires the clips to still exist, be findable, and be comparable, which is the part that usually falls apart.

Tag submissions by movement and date, and keep the camera setup fixed so the comparison is fair. A visible before and after is the single most effective retention tool a remote coach has, and it costs nothing beyond being organised from the start.

Close the loop

Feedback that does not get confirmed is feedback you cannot rely on. Ask for the next session's video with an explicit note about whether the cue made a difference. If the athlete cannot tell, that is information too: it usually means the cue was too abstract.

Common questions

What should an athlete film for a remote form check?

The full working set, filmed square to the side at hip crease height from three to four metres back, from the same marked spot every session, with the load and rep count noted.

How do you give useful form feedback over video?

Pin the feedback to a specific frame rather than describing it in general terms, draw on the frame where possible, and give one cue per review rather than a list of faults.

How often should a remote coach review video?

Enough to catch drift but not so often that the athlete stops self assessing. Weekly on the main lifts is a common cadence, with a full comparison review at the start and end of each training block.
