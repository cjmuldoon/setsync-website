How to Compare Two Lifts Side by Side on Video

11 October 2026 · 6 min read

Comparing today's lift against an older one is the fastest way to see whether a technique change actually took. It is also easy to do badly, in ways that make two different lifts look identical or two identical lifts look different.

Sync on an event, not on the start of the clip

This is the mistake that ruins most comparisons. If you start both videos at frame zero, they drift apart immediately, because you did not start recording at exactly the same moment relative to the lift and the two reps did not take the same amount of time.

Instead, pick a single unambiguous event in both clips and align on that. Good anchor points:

  • The moment the bar leaves the rack or the floor
  • The lowest point of the descent
  • The moment of lockout

Compare like for like

A comparison only means something if the only variable that changed is the one you are investigating. Before you read anything into a side by side, check that both clips share:

  • The same camera position, height and distance
  • A similar load, or at least a known difference in load
  • The same rep in the set, since rep one and rep five of the same set look different for reasons that have nothing to do with technique
  • The same footwear, since a change of shoe heel changes squat mechanics

If the camera moved between sessions, the comparison shows you a camera change. That is the single most common false positive.

Side by side or overlay

Both views answer different questions.

Side by side keeps each video readable and is better for comparing timing, rhythm and overall shape. It is the right default, and the one to use when showing a lifter their own video.

Overlay, where one clip is made semi transparent on top of the other, is better for spotting small positional differences such as bar position over mid-foot or depth. It only works if the camera was in the same place for both clips, otherwise you are overlaying two different perspectives and the differences you see are artefacts.

What is actually worth comparing

Vague comparisons produce vague conclusions. Decide what you are looking at before you press play:

  • Depth, judged at the lowest frame of each clip
  • Bar path shape and horizontal drift
  • The timing of the hip rise relative to the chest out of the bottom
  • Where in the rep the bar slows down
  • Joint angles at a matched point in the lift

Pick one. A comparison that tries to look at everything tends to confirm whatever you already believed.

Use more than two when it helps

Two clips show you a difference. Three or four show you a trend, which is usually the more useful thing. Lining up the same lift at four points across a training block makes it obvious whether a change is real or whether you cherry picked two reps that happened to differ.

Common questions

How do you sync two lifting videos of different lengths?

Align them on a shared event rather than on the start of the clip. Pick an unambiguous moment such as the bar leaving the floor or the bottom of the descent, and set that as the sync anchor in both videos.

Is overlay or side by side better for comparing lifts?

Side by side is better for timing and overall shape and works even if the camera moved. Overlay is better for small positional differences such as depth and bar position, but only if both clips were filmed from the identical camera position.

How many lifts can you compare at once?

Two is the common case, but comparing three or four across a training block is more informative because it shows a trend rather than a single difference. SetSync supports up to four.
