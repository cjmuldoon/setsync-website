Bar Path Explained: What a Good Deadlift and Squat Look Like

11 October 2026 · 8 min read

Bar path is the line the barbell traces through space during a lift. It is one of the few things in strength training that is genuinely objective: the bar either travelled in a straight line over mid-foot or it did not, and no amount of feel changes the trace.

Why the line matters

Every centimetre the bar travels horizontally is work that does not move the weight upward. Worse, horizontal drift puts the bar away from your base of support, which means your body has to generate extra force just to stop the lift tipping over.

The reference line is a vertical through the middle of the foot. That is where the bar wants to be, because that is where the system is balanced. A lift that drifts forward out of that line is a lift that is leaking force.

What a good deadlift bar path looks like

Close to a straight vertical line, with a slight backward drift as the bar passes the knees.

Common faults show up as recognisable shapes:

  • A forward loop off the floor means the hips rose before the bar did, and you are now pulling around your own knees
  • A pronounced S curve means the bar came away from the legs mid-pull and had to be dragged back in to lock out
  • A hitch at the top shows as a flat spot followed by a second acceleration

The useful thing about a trace is that it dates the fault. You can see exactly which frame the bar left the line, which usually tells you what to cue.

What a good squat bar path looks like

Almost perfectly vertical. The squat is the lift where the ideal is closest to a straight line, because the bar is fixed on your back and your job is to keep it over mid-foot throughout.

Forward drift in the descent usually means the knees are travelling before the hips. Forward drift out of the hole is the classic good morning squat, where the hips rise faster than the chest and the lift turns into a stiff legged deadlift with a bar on your back.

Horizontal drift as a number

Looking at a trace is useful. Measuring it is better, because it turns an impression into something you can track across a training block.

Drift is typically expressed as a percentage: the widest horizontal deviation relative to the total vertical travel of the bar. A squat at two percent drift is tight. One at eight percent has a real problem that is costing kilos.

Do not chase a perfect line

A dead straight bar path is not automatically the strongest lift for a given body. Limb proportions change what is optimal, and a tall lifter with long femurs will never produce the same trace as a short one.

The honest use of bar path is comparative. Compare your trace today against your trace at the same weight six weeks ago, or your third rep against your first. A path that degrades as the set goes on tells you where technique fails under fatigue, and that is far more actionable than measuring yourself against an idealised line you were never built to trace.

Common questions

What is bar path in weightlifting?

Bar path is the line the barbell travels through space during a lift, viewed from the side. It is used to judge efficiency: the straighter the line over mid-foot, the less wasted horizontal work.

Should a deadlift bar path be perfectly straight?

Close to straight, with a slight backward drift past the knees. A forward loop off the floor is the most common fault and usually means the hips rose before the bar.

How do you measure bar path drift?

As a percentage of horizontal deviation against total vertical bar travel. Two to three percent is tight. Anything approaching eight percent indicates a technique fault worth addressing.
